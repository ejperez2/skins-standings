Is the new NBA lottery making tanking worse?

A response to Sporting News' "NBA's anti-tanking rules are not having the desired effect" (Nov 5, 2026), using every regular-season game from 2018-19 to 2026-27 (games through Nov 4, 2026; 2020-21 omitted).

Short answer: no evidence the rules are making tanking worse. And comparing this November with last November can't tell either way.

  • The bottom of the league really is unusually deep right now, even after adjusting for luck.
  • That depth comes mostly from teams expected to be average, and the teams projected to be bad are playing exactly as expected.
  • Tanking under the old rules showed up after the trade deadline. The first test of the new rules is February–April 2027.

Why November vs. November can't detect tanking

Tanking happens later in the season. Under the old lottery, a team's reason to lose depended on where it stood in the standings. Once it was out of the play-in race, every extra loss improved its draft odds. In late October, every team still has 74+ games to play, so nobody is out of anything yet.

That's why the article's own "tanking 10" mostly weren't tanking in November 2025. The Bulls were 6-1 and the Bucks 5-3. The tanking came later: veterans traded at the February deadline, healthy starters held out, fourth quarters handed to rookies.

So the article is comparing two stretches with no tanking in them. November 2025 (old rules) and November 2026 (new rules) are both mostly tank-free. Whatever separates them is roster talent, injuries, offseason moves and luck. None of that is a response to the lottery, because the lottery wasn't shaping anyone's behavior in either November.

The rules could be working perfectly or failing completely, and these November numbers would look the same. A test that gives the same answer either way can't tell you which is true.

The rules change decisions, not rosters. The article measures success by whether bad teams win more. The lottery can only change what front offices choose to do: whom to trade, whom to rest, who plays the fourth quarter. It can't make a bad roster good.

A team that goes 1-7 while trying to win tells you about its roster, not its incentives. In November, teams were already trying to win under the old rules, so there was nothing there for the new rules to fix.

One kind of tanking could show up in November. That's "roster tanking": building a weak team on purpose over the summer. If the new rules had encouraged it, teams projected to be bad would be underperforming their preseason lines, or the preseason lines would be more spread out than usual. Neither has happened (see below).

What would detect it. Compare February–April 2027 with the same stretch in earlier seasons, using the change at the deadline above as the yardstick. If the reform works, 2027's drop should be close to zero, against −2.4 to −3.0 points per game for the bottom five in 2021-22, 2024-25 and 2025-26.

Three supporting checks would also help:

  • fourth-quarter fades by bottom teams in close games
  • bottom teams' win% in close games
  • how often healthy starters sit late in the season

A blanket incentive: where would it show?

If every non-playoff team gets the same push to win, overall win percentages may not move much. Bad teams play each other often (about 50 games after each recent deadline), and every one of those games has one winner and one loser. If both teams try harder, the record between them still nets to zero.

That leaves three places an effect can show up:

  • Games against everyone else. If bad teams stop giving games away, they should hold up better against good teams after the deadline.
  • Fourth quarters. Whether starters are on the floor late in close games (chart above).
  • Who they beat. A team trying to escape the bottom three gains most from beating its direct rivals.

The rules aren't fully blanket, either. The three worst teams now get fewer lottery balls than teams 4th–10th from the bottom. Play-in seeds 9–10 get fewer than teams just below them, so a borderline team still has a small reason to slide out of the play-in.

Under the old rules, did bad teams fall away against everyone else after the deadline? It depends on how you pick the bad teams.

  • Picked by preseason betting lines (known before the season, so 2026-27 can be tracked now): the drop is small and inconsistent. From 2021-22 on it was −0.4 to −1.7 points per game, and in 2024-25 the group actually improved (+0.7). Many of the teams that end up tanking weren't expected to be bad (in 2025-26: the Bucks, Grizzlies and Mavericks), and some expected-bad teams surprised and never tanked.
  • Picked on deadline day by their record so far (known in February, not now): the drop is clearer, −1.4 to −2.8 from 2021-22 on, except 2022-23. Some of these teams were unlucky before the deadline, so luck alone should lift them a little afterward. That means the drop, if anything, understates the change in behavior.

For 2027, the deadline-day version is the sharper test. The preseason version lets you follow this season's expected bottom ten from now on. So far they are at −5.7 points per game against the rest, in line with earlier seasons' preseason bottom ten before the deadline (−4.7 to −7.8).

Is this season's spread real or noise?

Both. After ~8 games a lot of the spread is luck. To see how much, I took each earlier season's full-season team ratings and played them out over this season's actual first 121 games, 14,000 times in all.

A spread as wide as this season's came up in 15% of those runs, so it's unusual but not exceptional. "Six teams with one win or fewer" came up only 1% of the time, but that's the statistic the article chose after seeing the standings.

The extra blowouts are not only about the worst teams, either. Leave out every game involving the six worst teams, and the rate of 20-point games is still the highest of the eight seasons. It continues a rise that began in 2024-25, before the new rules:

One thing the article gets right: the bottom tail is genuinely deep. With luck adjusted out, this season's five worst teams are at about −9.4 points per game. That is deeper than any earlier season's opening stretch (−2.6 to −7.3) and close to a typical post-deadline stretch. It's real; it just isn't tanking.

Who's at the bottom?

Preseason betting lines were no more spread out this year than in the previous two. The ten teams the market expected to be worst are playing slightly better than their lines.

Of the seven teams losing by 9+ points per game, four were expected to be around average: the Jazz, Hornets, Suns and Magic. Of the other three, the Kings and Bucks are a few points worse than their low lines, and the Bulls are far worse. Nobody suspects Orlando or Phoenix of tanking.

Fourth-quarter fades

Tanking at its most visible meant sitting healthy starters in the fourth quarter of close games; the Jazz were fined $500,000 for it. This test takes games within 8 points after three quarters and asks how bottom-10 teams did in the fourth quarter against everyone else.

Bottom 10 here means the ten lowest preseason win totals, so the same rule applies to every season, this one included. The biggest fade in the data is 2025-26's post-deadline stretch (−3.0 points, close-game win% .30). This season's early figure (−1.7) is in the normal early-season range, though it rests on only 22 games.

Data: ESPN game results, including quarter scores. Preseason lines: consensus win totals as of Oct 20, 2026 (and earlier-season equivalents stored in this repo).

Method: "Luck adjusted" subtracts the spread that a 13-point per-game random variation would produce (13² ÷ games played). For the bottom-five charts, it shrinks each team's margin toward the league average by how much of its spread is likely luck.
